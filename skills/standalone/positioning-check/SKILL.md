## Purpose

PMs now co-own positioning, and most positioning drafts fail the same way.
The team wrote them from the inside. The "competitor" is the vendor the team
watches, while the buyer was choosing between your product and a spreadsheet.
The "differentiator" is a feature the team is proud of, with no line drawn to
the value a buyer would pay for.

This skill checks a draft against Dunford's five components in her order and
reports which ones the PM can back with evidence. It does NOT write the
positioning statement, the tagline, the homepage hero, or the messaging
hierarchy. The PM writes those after closing the gaps.

## Procedure

1. Read the draft and any evidence the PM pasted. Restate the positioning in
   one plain sentence. If you cannot, say so. That is the first finding.
2. Extract the **competitive alternatives** the draft implies. Ask what a
   best-fit customer would do if this product did not exist. Include "do
   nothing", "hire someone", and "use a spreadsheet" when the evidence or the
   product type makes them plausible. Mark each alternative as taken from the
   PM's evidence or inferred by you.
3. List the **unique attributes** the draft claims. For each, test it against
   every alternative from step 2. An attribute an alternative also has is not
   unique. Say which ones fail.
4. For each surviving attribute, trace the **value** the draft says it
   produces for the buyer. Flag any attribute with no stated value, and any
   value claim with no attribute behind it.
5. Identify the **best-fit customer** the draft names. Check that the draft
   says what makes this customer care about the value more than others do. A
   segment defined only by size or industry is a flag.
6. Identify the **market category** the draft puts the product in. Name what
   that category makes a buyer assume about price, features and competitors,
   and whether those assumptions help or hurt the draft's own claims.
7. Sort every component into evidence-backed, opinion, or missing. Evidence
   means the PM pasted a customer quote, a deal note, or usage data that
   supports it.
8. Emit the output contract. Do not propose replacement copy.

## Output contract

Emit exactly these five sections, in this order.

### Positioning as read
The one-sentence restatement, or the statement that the draft does not yet
reduce to one sentence and why.

### Component check
One block per component, in Dunford's order (competitive alternatives, unique
attributes, value, best-fit customers, market category). Each block states
what the draft says, the status (evidence-backed, opinion, or missing), and
the specific problem if there is one.

### Weakest link
The single component that would break the positioning first, with the
reasoning. One component, not a list.

### What the alternative would say
For the strongest alternative from step 2, the honest two or three lines a
buyer would hear in its favor. This is the objection the positioning has to
survive.

### Conversations that would settle it
Up to five specific customer or lost-deal conversations, each tied to the
component it would move from opinion to evidence, with the one question to
ask.

End with one line: "The positioning is yours to write. Re-run this after the
conversations, with the quotes pasted in."

## Guardrails

- NEVER write or rewrite the positioning statement, tagline, headline, or any
  customer-facing copy. Quote the draft and name the problem. If the PM asks
  for "a better version", decline and point to the weakest link.
- Do not invent competitors, customer quotes, segments, or market categories.
  An alternative you inferred carries the label "inferred, confirm with
  customers".
- Do not grade a component as evidence-backed because the draft sounds
  confident. Only pasted evidence counts. With no evidence pasted, every
  component is opinion or missing, and the output says that plainly.
- A new model release is not a reason to reposition. If the draft's argument
  is "we now use a better model", flag that the alternatives will have it
  too.
- Keep the check about this draft. No general positioning advice the PM could
  find in the book.
